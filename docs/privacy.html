Vox

Privacy policy

Last updated: 2 October 2026

Vox is a free, open-source Windows and Android app for voice dictation and meeting notes. It has no analytics, no accounts and no server of its own. The developer does not receive, collect or store any of your data. Your audio and text go only to servers you choose, as described below.

Where your data goes

Vox has three kinds of server, and you pick every one of them in Settings:

  • Your speech server turns audio into text. By default this is Groq (groq.com) with your own API key.
  • Your cleanup server tidies the text. By default this is also Groq.
  • Your relay is an optional small server that you run yourself. It is off by default.

You can pick another provider (for example OpenAI, OpenRouter, Together or Mistral) or enter the address of your own OpenAI-compatible server (for example Whisper on your own computer, over your local network or Tailscale). Plain http is only accepted for this device, your local network and Tailscale addresses. Any other server must use https. Each server receives only its own key. What each server provider does with data is covered by that provider's own privacy policy and terms.

If cleanup is switched off, or the style is Raw, or the text is shorter than the minimum word count in Settings, the text of that dictation is not sent to the cleanup server. Voice notes are saved without an app name: the cleanup server is not told which app you were in.

The text a snippet types (Dictionary page) is put in after the cleanup, so it is never sent to the cleanup server; Improve my cleanup sends the phrase you said instead of it. On Windows, in a code editor or terminal (code mode, recognised from the program's file name of the window in front; nothing else of the editor is read for it), what you say is not sent to the cleanup server at all unless you chose AI cleanup for code apps in Settings (edit by voice excepted, see below).

On Windows, keep listening (double-tap the shortcut) sends what you say to the same servers in pieces of up to 20 seconds while you speak; the text goes to the cleanup server when you stop (a note) or piece by piece (typing into an app, with the program's file name). While it runs, the audio is also written to %APPDATA%\Vox\listen on your PC so that a crash does not lose it. It is removed when the session ends cleanly; after a failed send or a crash it stays, unencrypted, until you recover it or delete it.

On Windows, edit by voice (experimental, off until you set its shortcut) sends the text you selected and your spoken instruction to the cleanup server, also in a code editor whatever the code-mode setting says (it does not work in a terminal), and the answer replaces the selection. To read the selection Vox presses Ctrl+C, so the app you are in copies it to the clipboard the normal way: Windows can keep that copy in its clipboard history (Win+V) and, if clipboard sync across your devices is on in Windows, upload it to the Microsoft cloud clipboard, whatever Vox's clipboard settings say. Vox then puts your earlier clipboard back. The edited text is pasted through the clipboard like a dictation and follows the same two clipboard settings (below).

On Windows, the setting Keep the microphone ready (off by default) keeps the microphone open so that the first word of a dictation is not lost. While you are not recording, the sound is held only in memory, for the last 0.4 seconds, and is continuously overwritten: it is not saved to disk and not sent anywhere. When you start a dictation, those 0.4 seconds become the start of that recording and go to your speech server with it. Windows shows its microphone-in-use icon all the time while the setting is on. Turning the setting off, choosing another microphone or quitting Vox closes the microphone.

On Windows, a recording longer than about 7 seconds is sent to the speech server in pieces while you are still speaking (switch stream_stt, on by default). Short recordings are sent as one piece. To Groq and OpenAI the audio goes as FLAC, a lossless compression made on your PC (Settings, Audio format; WAV to every other server). Android does the same, and a clip of 4 seconds or more is compressed (AAC, in an m4a file) before it is sent to the same speech server. The compression happens on your phone.

Your relay (optional)

A relay is a small program (relay/relay.py) that you run on your own machine, for example a Raspberry Pi on your Tailscale network. Vox only talks to a relay if you turn one of these switches on, or press Test next to the relay address or Refresh on the Devices card, and you have entered its address and token. Sync, Test and Devices requests carry the name of your device. The Devices card asks the relay which devices have used it and shows each one's name, whether it is this device and when it was last seen.

The relay is yours, so what it stores is up to you. It keeps synced notes and the profile in a database file, and the AI server keys you give it in plain text in relay.json (readable only by its owner on Linux and macOS). Anyone who can read those files, or who has your relay token and can reach the relay, can read your notes and profile, and any API keys you chose to sync. Keep the relay on your own private network.

Data stored on your devices

On Windows your settings, dictation history, dictionary, meeting transcripts and notes are stored on your PC, in %APPDATA%\Vox and in Documents\Vox Notes. API keys and the relay token are protected by your Windows login. Voice notes are in notes.db in the same folder. They are not encrypted. In Settings you can turn dictation history off, and then nothing you dictate is saved to history. By default Vox puts your earlier clipboard back after pasting a dictation (text, images and files; from a very large one, over 64 MB, only its text). The Keep dictation on the clipboard setting (keep_clipboard) is off by default. On Windows the Keep dictations in Windows clipboard history (Win+V) setting (clipboard_history) is on by default: Windows may then keep each dictation in its clipboard history until Windows clears it; turn it off to keep dictations out of that history. A dictation is never uploaded to the cloud clipboard (the selection that edit by voice copies is the exception, see above). The copy-last shortcut follows the same setting. On both Windows and Android each history entry also keeps how long each step of that dictation took (waiting for the microphone, speech to text, cleanup, typing) and the names of the models used; the Speed card shows them. This stays on your device: it is not sent anywhere and not synced, and it goes when you delete the history entry.

Learn from my corrections (Windows and Android, on by default; switch it off in Settings > Privacy, "Learn from my corrections"): for up to 3 minutes after Vox types something, or until you send it, switch to another app or the typed text is gone, Vox reads the text of the field that has the focus in the window or app it typed into, to notice the words you fix. That is usually the field it typed into, but it can be another field there or, on Windows, the whole text of a web page, up to 20,000 characters; the watch ends as soon as what it reads no longer holds the text Vox typed. Whatever else you type in that field is read too. On Windows it reads the focused control every 2 seconds through Windows UI Automation, also in code editors and terminals; on Android it reads the field each text change in that app comes from, through its accessibility service, which asks for text changes only while it watches. Password fields are never read. Only the changed words are kept (numbers, half-typed words and a swap of one everyday word for another, such as their and there, are not learned): they are added to your dictionary (for example you vrag => Yuvraj) and to a list of your last 20 learned fixes on the Dictionary page, where you can remove them. The text of the field itself is held only in memory while Vox watches and is then dropped; it is never saved, logged or sent anywhere. The learned words then travel like the rest of your dictionary: as spelling hints to your speech and cleanup servers with every dictation, and to your relay if sync is on.

On Android, settings, voice notes and history are in the app's private storage. The API key, the relay token and the history are not encrypted on the phone. They are removed when you uninstall the app. The app lists the apps installed on the phone so the Styles page can offer them; this list never leaves the phone.

Uninstalling Vox and deleting the folders above removes the data on that device. It does not remove copies on a relay or on the servers of your providers.

Google Calendar (optional)

If you choose Connect Google Calendar, Vox requests read-only access to your calendar events (scope calendar.events.readonly) and your email address. Vox reads event titles, start and end times, attendee names and email addresses, the organizer, and video-call links, for the next 7 days. Vox uses this only to:

  • show your upcoming meetings in the app,
  • remind you (or start notes, if you turn that on) when a meeting begins,
  • title your notes and label who said what.

Calendar data is stored only on your PC. For meetings you choose to record, the meeting title, attendee names and organizer, and your own name from Settings, are sent to your servers as described in the table above. Vox does not modify your calendar, does not share Google user data with anyone else, does not use it for advertising, and does not use it to train AI models.

Vox's use and transfer of information received from Google APIs adheres to the Google API Services User Data Policy, including the Limited Use requirements.

To disconnect, click Disconnect in Vox > Notes > Calendar, or remove Vox at myaccount.google.com/permissions.

Recording other people

Meeting notes record what others say. Tell participants before you record. Recording laws differ by place.
